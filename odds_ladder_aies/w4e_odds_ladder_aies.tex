\authors{Dali Wang\correspondingauthor{Dali Wang, wangd@ornl.gov}\aff{a},

\abstract{Reservoir operations, hydropower, and cooling water depend on regional temperature, precipitation, and runoff at leads of weeks to years.
The atmospheric initial condition is largely lost at these leads, and the useful quantity is a shift in the probability of a hot, cold, wet, or dry period.
Earth system foundation models are commonly accepted on the root mean square error of the forecast field.
A squared error is minimized by the mean field, so a model can train successfully and still leave event probabilities at their historical rate.
This paper specifies an evaluation ladder for those probabilities before training.
Each rung changes one design axis: the target, the season, the lead, the variable, or the forecast object.
Training years alone fix the category boundaries and the historical class frequencies.
A claim is kept only when the Brier skill score is positive on every held-out sample.
The same events are scored over the Tennessee Valley in ERA5 and in E3SM seasonal-to-decadal hindcasts initialized on 1~May and 1~November.
In ERA5, winter precipitation probabilities remain skillful to a lead of two seasons, and one-week air temperature and surface runoff probabilities are skillful when the issue-week atmosphere is included.
In the hindcasts, one-week temperature skill recurs for November initializations; week-ahead runoff and winter precipitation do not.
The claim tables are the milestone control for a later model, on the leads this sample defends.
A row marked yes is an advance only when that model beats the logistic with the same issue-time information, on the same edges and the same held-out splits, in the dataset where it was trained.
A row marked thin or no uses the historical class rate as its floor.
Beating that rate is a separate result from beating the logistic.
The scores are point estimates.
A pass in one dataset is not a pass in the other, and no row covers a lead of a year.}

\sigstatement
This paper proposes evaluation criteria for weeks-to-years prediction of water and heat for energy systems.
At those leads, reservoir, hydropower, and cooling-water decisions depend on whether a region will be unusually hot, cold, wet, or dry, and deterministic scores of the forecast field can favor the historical pattern without shifting those probabilities.
The criteria form an evaluation ladder: one element of the forecast design changes at a time, and a result is retained only when the Brier skill score is positive on every held-out sample against a historical rate fixed on training years.
Applied to ERA5 and to the E3SM seasonal-to-decadal hindcast campaign over the Tennessee Valley, the ladder is the milestone control for a later foundation model: an advance only on a row marked yes, against the logistic with the same issue-time information, and only in the dataset where that model is trained.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Weeks-to-years prediction of water and heat is the lead at which an energy system plans.
In the Tennessee Valley, reservoir operations, hydropower scheduling, and the cooling water available to thermal generation depend on rainfall, streamflow, and river temperature over the coming season and the coming year.
Fuel procurement and maintenance are set on that same horizon.
The decisions sit past the reach of medium-range weather guidance and short of a climate projection~\citep{vitart2017s2s,saha2014cfsv2}.
The products that do cover this range, including operational seasonal forecasts out to about a year, are issued largely as anomalies from a historical weather pattern~\citep{saha2014cfsv2,johnson2019seas5}.
When hot, dry, and high-flow seasons depart from that pattern, the quantity an operator can use is a change in the probability of the event, relative to the rate the event already has.
Near-surface temperature, precipitation, and runoff are the variables through which this paper poses that question for the valley.

The prediction is hard because the information that makes a short forecast skillful does not survive to these leads, and the climate mean is not yet a seasonal decision.
The atmospheric initial condition is largely lost within weeks~\citep{vitart2017s2s}.
What remains is memory in the coupled system, including the El Ni\~no--Southern Oscillation and its effect on North American precipitation, and that memory appears as a shift in the probability of a regional class~\citep{ropelewski1986enso,hong2025enso}.
Physics-based systems built for the interval therefore use initialized hindcasts of about one to two years and score seasonal anomalies after a lead-dependent model climatology is removed.
The Climate Forecast System, SEAS5, and the CESM Seasonal-to-Multiyear Large Ensemble follow this design.
The last of these starts two-year hindcasts four times a year because seasonal protocols stop too soon, and decadal protocols start too seldom, to sample the interval~\citep{saha2014cfsv2,johnson2019seas5,yeager2022smyle}.
Regional skill in those systems remains limited, and the guidance that reaches an operator stays close to the historical pattern.

Foundation models are now trained on reanalysis and on coupled-model output to learn a predictive operator for Earth-system fields.
GraphCast, Pangu-Weather, and ClimaX are accepted on the root-mean-square error and the anomaly correlation of atmospheric maps at leads of hours to about ten days~\citep{lam2023graphcast,bi2023pangu,nguyen2023climax}.
ORBIT and ORBIT-2 carry that class of model toward Earth-system predictability and toward weather and climate downscaling~\citep{wang2024orbit,wang2025orbit2}.
Those scores ask whether the predicted map is close to the analysis.
A squared error is minimized by the mean field, so a model can train successfully by reproducing the historical pattern and still leave the probability of an unusual heat or water event at its historical rate.
A loss on the continuous amount averages that shift away, and additional capacity can fit weather noise while missing a shift that a small probability model still detects.
Seasonal and subseasonal systems already score categorical events with the Brier skill score~\citep{vitart2017s2s,kirtman2014nmme}.
That score has not been the acceptance test for a foundation model of regional water and heat at weeks-to-years leads.

This paper fixes that test before such a model is trained.
The evaluation ladder changes one design axis at a time: the target, the season, the lead, the variable, or the forecast object.
Category edges and the historical class rate are frozen on training years.
The score is the Brier skill score~\citep{brier1950,wilks2011}, and a result is retained only when the skill is positive on every pre-declared held-out split.
The same events are scored over the Tennessee Valley in ERA5~\citep{hersbach2020era5} and in the E3SM seasonal-to-decadal hindcast campaign, initialized on 1~May and 1~November for about 24 months~\citep{golaz2022e3sm}.
A pass in the reanalysis is evidence about the observed record.
A pass in the hindcasts is evidence about the model's own categories.
One result is not copied onto the other.
Section~\ref{sec:milestone} is the rule a later model is scored under.
The leads it can defend are the rows of Table~\ref{tab:claims}.
A lead of a year is not among them.

\section{Evaluation Ladder}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{One axis at a time}
\label{sec:axis}

Each rung changes one of five things: the target type (a continuous amount or a category), the season, the lead, the variable, or the forecast object (a regional mean or a map).
Predictors are a menu declared before the rung is scored.
A failure under that menu is a result for the menu.
It is not a license to search for a new predictor on the same test sample and then quote the original experiment.

\subsection{The event}
\label{sec:event}

The scored object is a regional-mean category.
For a water variable the classes are dry, near, and wet.
For temperature they are cool, near, and hot.
The two tercile edges, and the historical frequency of each class, are estimated on training time only and then frozen.
Validation and test samples are labeled with those edges.
They do not move the edges.

\subsection{The score}
\label{sec:score}

Let $p_{k,c}$ be the forecast probability of class $c$ at occasion $k$, and let $o_{k,c}$ be $1$ if that class occurs and $0$ otherwise.
The Brier score and its skill against the training class frequencies $\bar{o}_c$ are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{BS} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{c}(p_{k,c}-o_{k,c})^{2},\\
\mathrm{BSS} &= 1 - \mathrm{BS}/\mathrm{BS}_{\mathrm{ref}},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:bss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{BS}_{\mathrm{ref}}$ uses $p_{k,c}=\bar{o}_c$~\citep{brier1950,wilks2011}.
A positive skill score means the probabilities beat that historical rate.
Hit rate and the Heidke skill score~\citep{heidke1926} are reported beside it.
A claim requires $\mathrm{BSS}>0$ on every pre-declared held-out split, for that variable and that lead.
A single season with a positive score does not replace the pooled score.
Section~\ref{sec:milestone} separates that clearance from an advance, and it keeps a thin positive off the numeric floor.
The probability model used to test the ladder is logistic regression.
It is large enough to express a shift in class probabilities and small enough that a pass is unlikely to be memorized weather.

\subsection{The baseline is declared per dataset}
\label{sec:baseline}

The historical rate in \eqref{eq:bss} is the training class frequency of the dataset being scored.
It is the baseline of that experiment.
It is not assumed to be a 30-year climate normal.
In the week and month rungs of the reanalysis case, the years that define the edges are 2010--2014, because those are the training years of the daily pack.
A new dataset names its own training years before any score is computed.
Using one normal for both a reanalysis and a coupled-model ensemble is a separate, named choice.
It is not the default here.

\subsection{Two worlds, one protocol}
\label{sec:worlds}

Observation samples and coupled-model samples may use different year lists, different ensemble sizes, and different variable names.
They use the same event definition, the same edge rule, the same score, and the same pass rule.
A pass in one world is not written into the other world's table.

\subsection{What a map may add}
\label{sec:map}

A map is scored only after the regional-mean probability has cleared Section~\ref{sec:score}.
A pattern score does not rescue a failed regional mean.
A deterministic score of the map, such as its root-mean-square error, is a different experiment.
A smaller value of that score does not clear the probability row.
A higher regional-mean score than the logistic is an improvement on that probability.
It does not show that a spatial field contained information the regional predictors lacked.
Section~\ref{sec:milestone} is the rule that uses this distinction.

\subsection{Milestone rule for a later model}
\label{sec:milestone}

The ladder is frozen before a foundation model is trained.
It is the control for the leads that have been scored.
An unscored lead, including a lead of a year, has no floor in this paper.

A row is yes, thin, or no.
Yes requires Brier skill above zero on every pre-declared held-out split, with a margin the claim table treats as one the sample can carry.
Thin is a small positive Brier skill, or a positive Brier skill with a negative Heidke score.
No is every remaining row.
Where a rung also requires named season slices to be positive, a pooled score with a negative required slice is no.
A single season with a positive score does not replace the pooled score.

Clearance and advance are different results.
Clearance is $\mathrm{BSS}>0$ against the historical rate in \eqref{eq:bss} on every pre-declared split.
An advance is available only on a yes row.
It requires clearance, and a Brier score lower than the matched logistic on those same splits.
On a thin row and on a no row, the floor is the historical rate.
The recorded thin skill is kept in the table.
It is not the number a later model must beat.

The matched logistic is the pre-declared menu whose inputs the later model is allowed to use at issue time.
Indices alone, and indices plus the spatial mean of the issue-period atmosphere, are different menus.
A model that receives that atmosphere is scored against the atmosphere menu.
A model limited to the slow indices is scored against the indices-only menu.
When the atmosphere menu scores below the indices-only menu, the atmosphere menu is not the control.
A menu chosen by searching the same test sample after the rung is closed is not a control.

The same held-out rows fix the logistic and then receive the later model.
The comparison is paired and descriptive.
This paper does not compute a block bootstrap, a permutation test, or a minimum margin.
A yes row is a closed control on that split.
It is not a significance claim.
Years after the current test window lie outside the milestone.

The score is computed in the dataset the later model was trained on.
An ERA5 yes row is not an E3SM yes row.
A model scored against the hindcast's own terciles is not a forecast of the observed valley.
The edges remain the training ruler of Section~\ref{sec:baseline}.
A named climate normal, such as 1991--2020 or 1980--2009, is a new scorecard.
Tercile classes are the event this milestone scores.
A reservoir or cooling-water threshold is a different event.
Reliability of the issued probabilities is not part of the milestone.
Where inside the valley the event sits, against a climatological probability map, is a separate experiment.

\section{Reanalysis Case}
\label{sec:case}

\subsection{Region and series}
\label{sec:data}

The case is the Tennessee Valley in ERA5~\citep{hersbach2020era5}.
Seasonal rungs use a monthly index pack: autumn Pacific indices for winter precipitation, and autumn Atlantic and Arctic indices for winter air temperature, with training seasons through 2007 and 15 test seasons in 2009--2023.
Week and month rungs use daily fields on the box $34.0^{\circ}$--$37.8^{\circ}$N, $90.5^{\circ}$--$81.0^{\circ}$W.
The week split is 259 training weeks (2010--2014), 155 validation weeks (2015--2017), and 103 test weeks (2018--2019).
The month split holds out 2008 for validation and 2009--2023 for test.
These two year lists are not one sample.
Scored variables are 2~m air temperature, precipitation, and ERA5 surface runoff.
Air temperature is not river temperature.
Surface runoff is a reanalysis flux, not a stream gauge.
Figure~\ref{fig:domain} shows the seasonal predictors and the valley whose mean is the target.

\label{sec:week}

Summer and winter columns are slices of the test set.
They are reported so a pooled pass can be read by season.
They are not a substitute for the pooled pass.

\subsection{What the failures fix in the score}
\label{sec:failures}

The opening amount model used this month calendar and a masked squared error on standardized anomalies at leads of one to three months together.
It scored near the long-term average and stopped at the first epoch.
That run is why Tables~\ref{tab:season}--\ref{tab:month} score categories rather than amounts.
Same-day maps can be trained on this archive, so a map failure at a lead is not a pipeline failure.
Same-day surface runoff is readable from the atmosphere.
Top-layer soil moisture is not, and soil moisture was left off the probability gate.
Week-ahead rain maps and week-ahead rain probabilities fail together.
On the winter labels, extra capacity raised the one-season probability score once and lost to the logistic at two seasons.
The residual Transformer raised winter-precipitation Brier skill from $+0.091$ to $+0.140$ while matching the logistic hit rate and Heidke score.
That gain is a change in the probabilities on the same class calls.
It is not evidence that a spatial field contained information the regional predictors lacked.
The event definition, not the size of the network, is what separates the rows that pass from the rows that do not.

\section{Using the Ladder Again}
\label{sec:again}

\subsection{A later model in the same reanalysis}
\label{sec:later}

A later model trained on these fields is reduced to Table~\ref{tab:claims} under Section~\ref{sec:milestone}.
The reduction uses the same issue times, the same regional mean, and the same frozen edges.
The yes rows are winter precipitation at a lead of one season, one-week air temperature with the issue-week atmosphere, and one-week surface runoff with that same atmosphere.
On those rows an advance must beat the matched logistic.
The one-week temperature row is a pooled pass.
Its test-winter slice is negative, so it is not a winter temperature forecast.
Winter air temperature, fall precipitation, two-season winter precipitation, and two-week runoff are thin.
Their floor is the historical rate in \eqref{eq:bss}.
Temperature and precipitation at two and three weeks, precipitation at one week, and every month-ahead water row are no.
Their floor is the same historical rate.
The week-ahead rows and the seasonal rows are different events.
Passing one does not pass the other.
A weekly reference map that the model displays, including a multi-decade week-of-year mean, is not the tercile ruler of Section~\ref{sec:baseline}.
Scoring against a newly chosen normal is a new case.

\subsection{Coupled-model case}
\label{sec:e3sm}

The second case asks whether the claims in Table~\ref{tab:claims} still shift categories in the E3SM seasonal-to-decadal (S2D) hindcast campaign.
Starts are 1~May and 1~November in 1980--2003 and 2014--2018, under one model version.
Training starts are 1980--1995, validation is 1996--2003, and the test is 2014--2018, including 2015.
Each row is the mean of members EN00--EN02.
The members share the observed index, so they are not three samples.
Terciles are refit on the training starts, separately for May and November.
Week and month rungs then have 29 training starts, 16 validation starts, and 10 test starts.
A seasonal rung has about 15 training starts and 5 test starts.

Predictors that must be known before the start are the two preceding months: September and October for a November start, March and April for a May start.
The November value is not used, because that monthly index includes the rest of November.
Pacific predictors are the Oceanic Ni\~{n}o Index, Ni\~{n}o-3.4, and the Ni\~{n}o-3.4 box anomaly.
Temperature predictors at seasonal leads are the NAO and the AO.
The week-ahead atmosphere is days 1--7 of the forecast.
Temperature is 2~m air temperature.
Precipitation is total precipitation.
Runoff is total liquid runoff.
Daily land history does not store the surface-runoff flux used in the reanalysis, so the runoff rows are a related event, not the same field.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

Fifteen test seasons can support a research gate.
They cannot support an operational hit-rate claim.
The winter precipitation model rarely calls the dry class, so the probability score is the more stable summary.
Validation at the month lead is a single year.
The week split and the seasonal split cover different years, and a sentence that merges them is not supported.
The 2010--2014 terciles are the training ruler of the week and month rungs, not a named climate normal.
A failed season is a result for the pre-declared index menu.
The ensemble test is 10 starts for a week or a month and 5 starts for a season, so an ensemble pass is thinner than a reanalysis pass.
Ensemble runoff is total liquid runoff, not the reanalysis surface flux.
The ensemble score is against the model's own terciles, not against the observed valley.
The CFSv2 comparison is a Tennessee Valley categorical check on 15 winters~\citep{barnston2012cfsv2}.
It is not a global verification of that forecast system.
No block bootstrap is reported, so a thin positive is not a margin a later model can be required to exceed.
The held-out rows that fixed the logistic are the rows that will receive the later model.
That comparison does not use a fresh sample.
Equal terciles are not a reservoir or cooling-water threshold, and the reliability of the issued probabilities is not scored.
No lead of a year has a score, so the milestone does not cover that lead.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conc}

Weeks-to-years event probabilities are a hard forecast problem because a deterministic score of the field, such as the root-mean-square error, can be minimized by the reference field.
The ladder isolates the alternative quantity: a shift of category probabilities above a historical rate that was frozen before the test.
In ERA5 that filter keeps winter precipitation odds out to two seasons, one-week air-temperature and surface-runoff odds when the issue week's atmosphere is included, and a thinner two-week runoff odds from those same predictors.
In the S2D hindcasts the same rule keeps one-week temperature odds for November initializations and does not keep the week-ahead runoff odds or the winter precipitation odds.
A later model is an advance only on a yes row, only against the logistic with the same issue-time information, and only in the dataset where it was trained.
A thin row and a no row keep the historical rate as their floor.
Clearance of that rate is not the advance.
An unscored lead, including a lead of a year, is outside the milestone.
